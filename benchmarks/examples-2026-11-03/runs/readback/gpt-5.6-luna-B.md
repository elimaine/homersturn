F1 The recursive paragraph
When: When an ending or overture needs each sentence to begin on the same phrase and take it one step further.
Not: When repetition adds no new step or the frame would recur throughout the page.
Example: At the door, he waited. At the door, he listened. At the door, he heard her leave.
Misuse: Repeating the opening phrase while merely restating the same fact.

F2 The litany
When: When a scene or tic earns a prose list whose number of items belongs to the content.
Not: When three items are chosen by reflex or no scene, habit, or pressure calls for a list.
Example: She packed socks, the map, two oranges, and her father’s key.
Misuse: Forcing every list into three items or adding decorative objects without purpose.

F3 The withheld word
When: When the piece still holds open a word the reader has reached for more than once and will later pay.
Not: When the missing word has not been planted or the figure would merely obscure meaning.
Example: She reached for the — — —, but the lock opened from inside.
Misuse: Using three em dashes as decoration, hesitation, or a substitute for an unpaid reveal.

F4 The crescendo
When: When an evaluation must climb by degrees toward its earned final word.
Not: When the low first step is unnecessary or the adjectives do not genuinely increase the pressure.
Example: The apology was awkward, then cruel, then unforgivable.
Misuse: Stacking increasingly loud adjectives without making each stage stronger than the last.

F5 The revision
When: When the narrator can open a reading the reader holds and replace it in view with a better reading.
Not: When the first reading was never planted or the correction explains the telling instead of revising the matter.
Example: I took the light in the window for a welcome; then the curtain fell, and I read it as a warning.
Misuse: Adding a second description that enlarges the first instead of correcting it.

F6 The parallel
When: When two characters can receive the same beat in different keys and one deliberate difference can pay the shared pattern.
Not: When every beat mirrors, the narrator names the parallel, or the device becomes a habit.
Example: Mara hid the letter in her coat; at the station, Ivo hid the ticket in his shoe, but only Mara boarded.
Misuse: Giving two characters identical actions until they become one character with two names.

F7 The swear
When: When a shock has just landed and the swear belongs in the shocked person’s mouth, followed by silence.
Not: When the word is used for general emphasis, borrowed for the narrator, or explained afterward.
Example: The crib was empty, the window open. “Fuck.” No one moved.
Misuse: Having a character swear at every turn or explaining what the swear meant.

F8 The dash (with the semicolon, the comma-and, the participial tail and the parenthesis)
When: When a sentence must break off in silence just before the thing arrives, using at most one dash in a paragraph.
Not: When a dash, semicolon, comma-and, participial tail, or parenthesis merely joins clauses that should be separate.
Example: The envelope contained her father’s—
Misuse: Joining two complete thoughts with repeated dashes, semicolons, or parenthetical asides.

F9 The figure hums too
When: When a page needs its figures, moods, persons, and frames to vary rather than settle into one steady density.
Not: When a figure recurs in consecutive paragraphs, a frame is reused, or one device hums at a constant rate.
Example: A page uses one litany in the scene, then lets the next paragraph move plainly.
Misuse: Filling every paragraph with dashes, crescendos, or the same sentence frame.

S1 The character in the subject, the action in the verb
When: When the sentence should keep the actor visible and make the action clear.
Not: When the prose buries the actor in an abstract noun or passive construction.
Example: Mara opened the gate.
Misuse: The opening of the gate was effected by Mara.

S2 One thing, one name
When: When one term can name a thing throughout and each return should remain clear.
Not: When a new name has not earned its meaning as a payment.
Example: The contract remained the contract until her signature made it the surrender.
Misuse: Calling one object the proposal, the document, the package, and the plan merely to avoid repetition.

S3 The common word for the common thing
When: When an ordinary thing has an ordinary name that carries the meaning plainly.
Not: When the rare word is the only carrier or the common word is loaded by another domain.
Example: Use “house” for an ordinary house, not “dwelling.”
Misuse: Choosing “habitation” to sound important when “home” is right.

S4 The narrator’s opinion on the word, not in a trailing clause
When: When the narrator’s judgment can sit directly on the word or action being described.
Not: When the judgment is postponed to a trailing clause that explains what the reader already sees.
Example: The captain foolishly opened the door.
Misuse: The captain opened the door, which showed how foolish he was.

S5 Open a sentence on its subject
When: When the reader should meet the sentence’s subject immediately, unless the opening phrase cannot be mistaken for one.
Not: When a bare time noun or similar phrase will read as the subject before the real one arrives.
Example: Mara opened the shop at dawn.
Misuse: The morning after the fire, Mara opened the shop.

S6 One hedge per claim
When: When a claim needs uncertainty, use one hedge and let a number pay while an adjective raises.
Not: When several qualifiers are stacked around one claim or uncertainty is padded instead of specified.
Example: The bridge is probably 30 years old.
Misuse: The bridge may perhaps possibly be unsafe.

S7 The example after the claim, the definition before its use
When: When a reader needs a term defined before relying on it and an example to follow the claim.
Not: When an unexplained term appears first or an example is offered before the point it supports.
Example: A checksum is a stored value used to detect corruption. For example, one changed byte makes the comparison fail.
Misuse: “Use a checksum,” followed by its definition only after several paragraphs.

S8 Concede before you are forced; the title is the first raise
When: When the page can acknowledge the strongest objection early and let its title plant the first debt.
Not: When the objection is withheld until the reader forces it or the title raises nothing.
Example: Title: “The Fast Fix Is Not Free.” The essay then grants that it ships sooner before arguing against it.
Misuse: Pretending the opposing case does not exist until the final paragraph.

S9 A name is a plant at the scale of the cast
When: When a name can open a reading before the character speaks without the narrator pointing at its meaning.
Not: When the page glosses the name or other details announce the reading it is meant to plant.
Example: Mercy signed the receipt and asked them to count the change.
Misuse: Mercy, named for her kindness, forgave everyone in the room.

S10 A cliché is a refrain planted by other texts
When: When an exact detail or a character’s own phrase can carry the meaning instead of inherited language.
Not: When relying on a stock phrase, borrowed joke, plot, title, or frame that the page itself has not planted.
Example: Instead of “the calm before the storm,” write, “The gulls stopped before the wind arrived.”
Misuse: Calling a familiar situation “the calm before the storm” and treating the borrowed phrase as payment.
