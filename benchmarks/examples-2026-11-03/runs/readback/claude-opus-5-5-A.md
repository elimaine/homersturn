**F1 The recursive paragraph**
When: Once in a piece, at the end or as the overture, where each sentence can open on the same phrase and push it one step further.
Not: In the middle of a piece, a second time, or when the repeated phrase only restates and never advances.
Example: She kept the key. She kept the key after he left. She kept the key after the locks were changed, and still tried it on Sundays.
Misuse: Sentences that share an opening and stand still, or the device in paragraph after paragraph, so it hums instead of climbing.

**F2 The litany**
When: When a scene earns a list said as prose, with exactly as many items as the content has.
Not: As a tic in a post, or when you are rounding to three by reflex.
Example: He packed the stove, the two good knives, his mother's ledger, and nothing for the children.
Misuse: Three items every time because three sounds finished, padded with a third thing that was never there.

**F3 The withheld word**
When: Once, for a word the piece still holds open and will pay, after the reader has reached for it more than once.
Not: Before the reader has reached for the word, or for a word the piece never pays.
Example: Everyone in town knew what Ada had done, and nobody called it ———.
Misuse: Blanking a word nobody wanted or using it twice, so the withholding reads as coyness and the reader stops reaching.

**F4 The crescendo**
When: When the evaluative word should climb by degrees because the state is rising.
Not: When nothing is rising, or when you would skip the low first step.
Example: The soup was fine, then good, then the best thing she had eaten since the war.
Misuse: Starting at the top or cutting the low step, so there is no climb, only a superlative.

**F5 The revision**
When: When the reader holds a planted first reading that the narrator can replace in view, the second clause in the grammar of the first.
Not: When the first reading was never planted, when the correction is about the telling, or as the pivot in every paragraph.
Example: He told himself he came back for the money, and found he had come back for the dog.
Misuse: A "not X but Y" turn in every paragraph, denying readings nobody held, which is the figure humming.

**F6 The parallel**
When: Once a piece, when two characters can take the same beat in different keys, one beat left unmirrored and paid where their arcs meet.
Not: As a habit, carried by a line instead of beats, or named by the narrator.
Example: Both sisters pocket the brooch at the wake, and only the younger puts it back.
Misuse: Mirroring every beat until the two are one character with two names, or a narrator noting how alike they are.

**F7 The swear**
When: One hit on the line that lands, after a shock the reader just felt, in the mouth of the person it belongs to, then silence.
Not: Without a felt shock, in a mouth it does not belong to, or with anything after it that explains it.
Example: The vicar reads the scan, says "Fuck," and nobody speaks until the car.
Misuse: Swearing for color in every scene, or a follow-up line telling us she never swore, so this one mattered.

**F8 The dash (with the semicolon, the comma-and, the participial tail and the parenthesis)**
When: At most one dash a paragraph, where a sentence breaks off before the thing arrives, and one parenthesis a page for an aside under the breath.
Not: To join two clauses that should be two sentences, a job the semicolon, the comma-and and the participial tail also should not do.
Example: "If you open that door, I swear I will—" She opened it.
Misuse: "The rain stopped—the town exhaled," and its cousin "The rain stopped, the town exhaling," clauses stitched together on every line.

**F9 The figure hums too**
When: As a check on every page: each figure at most twice, never in consecutive paragraphs, with mood and person moving where the state moves.
Not: When the repeated frame is the refrain, the one return the skill allows, or when the rhythm is the content.
Example: On a six-paragraph page, only the second and fifth pivot on a word, and the rest turn by therefore or but.
Misuse: The same pivot in every paragraph, an aphorism closing each one, or a whole page in one mood and one person.

**S1 The character in the subject, the action in the verb**
When: In every sentence about who does what.
Not: The skill names no exception, so never trade the actor for a passive or the verb for a noun made from it.
Example: Mara signed the lease.
Misuse: "The signing of the lease was completed," where nobody acts and the verb is "completed."

**S2 One thing, one name**
When: Whenever a thing recurs: define the term once and use it throughout, so each return pays.
Not: When the new name is a payment, a rename that marks a change the reader should feel.
Example: The doc calls it "the cache" in its first section and still "the cache" in its last.
Misuse: "The cache," then "the store," then "the buffer layer," so the reader counts three things where there is one.

**S3 The common word for the common thing**
When: By default, for any ordinary thing.
Not: When the rare word is the only carrier, or the common word is loaded by another domain.
Example: She walked home.
Misuse: "Utilize," "commence" and "liminal" where "use," "start" and "between" carry it, the thesaurus worn as a costume.

**S4 The narrator's opinion on the word, not in a trailing clause**
When: When the narrator judges something: one adverb on the first description.
Not: In a judgement clause trailing the description, or as opinion on the telling instead of the matter.
Example: The mayor gave a heroically short speech.
Misuse: "He gave a speech, which, of course, said nothing at all," the aphorism in another costume, at the same spot on every page.

**S5 Open a sentence on its subject**
When: At the top of every sentence: the subject, or a phrase the reader cannot take for one.
Not: On a bare time noun that reads as the subject until the real one arrives.
Example: In March, the dam failed.
Misuse: "Monday the director quit," where "Monday" reads as the subject until "the director" forces a re-read.

**S6 One hedge per claim**
When: When a claim is uncertain: mark it once, and give a number where you can.
Not: Stacked hedges on one claim, or an adjective where a number exists.
Example: The fix probably cuts load time from four seconds to one.
Misuse: "This might arguably somewhat improve performance in some cases," which drains trust and says nothing.

**S7 The example after the claim, the definition before its use**
When: When you illustrate a claim or introduce a term: the claim, then the example; the definition a paragraph before the term is used.
Not: The example before its claim, or the definition arriving at or after the term's first use.
Example: Retries can make an outage worse; last May every client retried three times and tripled the database's load.
Misuse: An anecdote whose point surfaces later, or "the pool" used for three paragraphs before anyone says what the pool is.

**S8 Concede before you are forced; the title is the first raise**
When: When a weakness is real: concede it before a reader corners you, and treat the title as a promise the piece will pay.
Not: After the objection is unavoidable, or under a title that raises what the body never pays.
Example: An essay titled "Why I Stopped Grading" admits in its second paragraph that grades still help some students.
Misuse: The concession buried in the last line once it cannot be dodged, under a title promising something the body never delivers.

**S9 A name is a plant at the scale of the cast**
When: When naming a cast: names that bracket a span or share a sound link characters before either speaks.
Not: When anything on the page points at the link, or the narrator glosses the name.
Example: Ada enters on the first page and Zoe on the last, and no one remarks on the letters.
Misuse: "Her name was Grace, and grace was exactly what she lacked," a gloss that turns the plant into a label.

**S10 A cliché is a refrain planted by other texts**
When: Wherever a cliché offers itself: an exact detail instead, or a phrase borrowed from a character's mouth.
Not: A stock phrase, or a borrowed joke, plot, title or frame, unless the user proposed it, which makes it their plant to pay.
Example: Not "her heart sank" but "she set the letter face down and straightened it."
Misuse: "A rollercoaster of emotions," a plot lifted whole from a famous film, or a quoted example from a guide reused as a line.
