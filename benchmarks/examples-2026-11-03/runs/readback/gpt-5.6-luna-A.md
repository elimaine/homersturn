F1 The recursive paragraph
When: Use it once, at an ending or overture, when each sentence begins on the same phrase and advances it.
Not: Do not use it when repetition makes no progression or as a routine frame for every paragraph.
Example: The door was locked. The door was locked against the wind. The door was locked against us.
Misuse: Repeating the opening phrase without taking it one step further.

F2 The litany
When: Use a prose list whose number of items belongs to the scene or the post's content.
Not: Do not force three items by reflex or use a litany where a plain sentence is enough.
Example: He packed the passport, the flashlight, the spare key, and the photograph.
Misuse: Giving every list three items regardless of what the passage contains.

F3 The withheld word
When: Use three em dashes once, after the reader has reached for the word more than once and the piece will pay it.
Not: Do not withhold a word the reader has not been led to seek or that the piece will never recover.
Example: She reached for the— — —, and the avoided name rose in her throat.
Misuse: Using three dashes as decoration while offering no later word, clue, or payment.

F4 The crescendo
When: Use an evaluative word that climbs by necessary degrees, keeping the low first step that makes the climb.
Not: Do not stack intensifiers automatically or use a lone superlative with no earned escalation.
Example: The room was uneasy, then hostile, then murderous.
Misuse: Calling the room “good, great, perfect” without any change between the steps.

F5 The revision
When: Use it when the reader holds a first reading that the narrator replaces in view with a truer one about the matter.
Not: Do not correct an unplanted reading or let the second clause merely add information instead of replacing the first.
Example: I took her silence for fear; it was calculation.
Misuse: Writing “I thought X, but actually Y” when the reader never had reason to think X.

F6 The parallel
When: Use one arc-level parallel, giving two characters the same beat in different keys and one meaningful mismatch.
Not: Do not mirror every beat, name the comparison, or make the parallel a habit.
Example: Mara returned the borrowed coat clean; Eli returned the borrowed key bent.
Misuse: Having Mara and Eli perform identical actions in every scene until they become one character with two names.

F7 The swear
When: Use one swear in the mouth of the person who owns it after a shock the reader has just felt, followed by silence.
Not: Do not use it without shock, in the narrator's voice, or explain it afterward.
Example: The photograph showed his own face. “Fuck.”
Misuse: Repeating “fuck” as punctuation and then explaining that the character was angry.

F8 The dash (with the semicolon, the comma-and, the participial tail and the parenthesis)
When: Use the dash as one silent break before the thing arrives, and use the other joins or an aside only with the same deliberate restraint.
Not: Do not use them to join clauses that should be separate; allow at most one dash per paragraph and one parenthesis per page.
Example: She lifted the lid—and stopped.
Misuse: He opened the door—and she ran—and the dog barked, with a parenthesis in every paragraph.

F9 The figure hums too
When: Vary and space figures so each accents a turn instead of making the page uniformly dense.
Not: Do not repeat a frame, turn, mood, or person at a steady rate, in consecutive paragraphs, or more than twice a page.
Example: Only the two crisis paragraphs begin “By morning”; the intervening paragraphs do not.
Misuse: Beginning every paragraph with “By morning” until the frame becomes wallpaper.

S1 The character in the subject, the action in the verb
When: Put the character who acts in the subject and the action in the verb when agency matters.
Not: Do not hide the actor in a passive construction or nominalization when the character is the point.
Example: The nurse closed the window.
Misuse: The closing of the window was effected by the nurse.

S2 One thing, one name
When: Keep one name for one thing unless introducing a new name pays with a new meaning.
Not: Do not rename a thing merely to avoid repeating its established name.
Example: The permit stayed on the desk; I filed the permit at noon.
Misuse: Calling the same permit an authorization, document, paper, and clearance in successive sentences.

S3 The common word for the common thing
When: Use the common word when it carries the meaning, unless the rare word is the only carrier.
Not: Do not reach for a rare word when the common word is right or when the common word is not loaded by another domain.
Example: He walked to the house.
Misuse: He traversed the domicile to avoid saying “walked to the house.”

S4 The narrator's opinion on the word, not in a trailing clause
When: Put the narrator's judgment into the first description, often as one adverb or a charged word.
Not: Do not append a trailing judgment clause or comment on the telling itself.
Example: The mayor offered a regrettably cheerful toast.
Misuse: The mayor offered a cheerful toast, which showed how shallow he was.

S5 Open a sentence on its subject
When: Begin on the subject, or on a phrase the reader cannot mistake for one.
Not: Do not begin with a bare time noun or other phrase that temporarily reads as the subject.
Example: The courier dropped the package after the meeting.
Misuse: The morning after the meeting, the courier dropped the package.

S6 One hedge per claim
When: Use no more than one hedge for a claim, letting a number pay and an adjective raise.
Not: Do not stack words such as “may,” “perhaps,” and “somewhat” around the same claim.
Example: The bridge is probably unsafe.
Misuse: The bridge may perhaps be somewhat unsafe.

S7 The example after the claim, the definition before its use
When: State the claim first, put its example after it, and define a term before the paragraph that uses it.
Not: Do not make the example carry an unstated claim or define a term at the moment it is needed.
Example: The cache is brittle; yesterday, one missing header stopped the import.
Misuse: Yesterday, one missing header stopped the import, proving the cache is brittle; a retry budget is defined afterward.

S8 Concede before you are forced; the title is the first raise
When: Put the strongest obvious concession early and make the title raise the first promise.
Not: Do not hide the concession until rebuttal or use a title that raises nothing.
Example: Title: The Cheap Fix. The patch is ugly, but it removes the outage.
Misuse: The Cheap Fix promises simplicity, then admits several paragraphs later that the patch breaks the API.

S9 A name is a plant at the scale of the cast
When: Let names quietly carry a relation across the cast without having the narrator point to it.
Not: Do not gloss the name's meaning or direct the reader toward the pattern it plants.
Example: Mara hid the map; Maren returned with the route.
Misuse: Mara and Maren—whose shared root signaled their bond—entered together.

S10 A cliché is a refrain planted by other texts
When: Replace inherited phrases with exact details, or let a borrowed phrase belong to a character's mouth.
Not: Do not rely on a borrowed phrase, joke, plot, title, or frame to carry meaning merely because other texts planted it.
Example: Instead of “the calm before the storm,” write, “The birds stopped before the sirens began.”
Misuse: Repeating “the calm before the storm” as if the familiar phrase were itself a payment.
