F1 The recursive paragraph
When: at the end of a piece or as its overture, once, when each sentence can take the same opening phrase one step further.
Not: anywhere in the middle, or a second time on the same page, where it becomes a frame reused and the figure hums.
Example: "She kept the key. She kept the key through the move. She kept the key through the second marriage. She kept the key, and that was the whole of it."
Misuse: a paragraph where every sentence starts on the phrase but none goes further than the last, so it is a list wearing a refrain.

F2 The litany
When: in a scene that earns it, as a list said as prose with exactly as many items as the content has.
Not: in a post, where it reads as a tic, and never at three items because three came by reflex.
Example: "The drawer held a receipt, a ring, a photo of someone neither of them knew, and the lease."
Misuse: "He was tired, hungry, and done," three abstractions in a row because a sentence seemed to want a third.

F3 The withheld word
When: once in a piece, after the reader has reached for the word more than once, and only for a word the piece still holds open and will pay.
Not: before the reader has reached for the word, or for a word the piece never pays, or more than once a piece.
Example: "She had always called the house — well. She had always called it that."
Misuse: a dash-and-blank on the first mention, withholding a word nobody was reaching for, and the paragraph after it names the word anyway.

F4 The crescendo
When: when an evaluative word can climb by degrees across a passage and the lowest step is on the page first.
Not: when the low first step has been cut, since without it there is no climb, only the top note.
Example: "It was fine. By Tuesday it was good. By Friday he was telling strangers it was the best year he had ever had."
Misuse: opening on "the best year he had ever had," with no "fine" before it, so the superlative is an importance claim and not a step.

F5 The revision
When: at most twice a page, when the reader already holds a reading the narrator can replace in view, on the matter, with the second clause in the grammar of the first.
Not: in every paragraph as a pivot, on the telling instead of the matter, or before the first reading has been planted.
Example: "He had come to say goodbye, she thought, and then saw the suitcase was hers."
Misuse: "He had come to say goodbye — no, actually, to take her with him," in a paragraph where no reader had yet assumed goodbye.

F6 The parallel
When: once a piece, when two characters can be given the same beat in different keys and one beat is deliberately not mirrored, carried by beats and never by a line.
Not: when every beat mirrors, since that is one character with two names, or when the narrator names the pairing.
Example: Both brothers are handed the father's watch in their chapter; the elder winds it, the younger sells it, and nobody says the word "inheritance."
Misuse: "Like his brother before him, he too stood at that same window," the narrator pointing at the mirror, every beat matched.

F7 The swear
When: once, as a sforzando on the line that lands, earned by a shock the reader just felt, in the mouth of the person it belongs to, followed by silence.
Not: as seasoning across the page, from a mouth it does not belong to, or before the shock has landed on the reader.
Example: The door opens on the empty crib. "Fuck." Nothing else on the page.
Misuse: "'Fuck,' he said, because the sight of it was more than he could bear," the swear explained and the silence filled.

F8 The dash (with the semicolon, the comma-and, the participial tail and the parenthesis)
When: at most one dash a paragraph, only where a sentence breaks off before the thing arrives; the parenthesis once a page, as the aside under the breath.
Not: to join two clauses that should have been two sentences, which the semicolon, the comma-and and the participial tail also do and hum the same way.
Example: "She reached for the lamp and the lamp was —" and the next line is the neighbour's voice through the wall.
Misuse: "The lamp was on — she had left it on — which meant she had expected to come back, leaving him to wonder; and he did," four joins in one sentence and no silence anywhere.

F9 The figure hums too
When: when a figure has been used once on the page and the next use is more than a paragraph away and the frame is not being reused.
Not: when the same device returns at a steady rate, in consecutive paragraphs, with one verb mood and one person held across the whole page.
Example: A page where one paragraph turns on a word, three plain ones follow, and the refrain returns only at the close.
Misuse: every paragraph ends on an aphorism and pivots on a word in its last line, and "you" in the present tense from top to bottom.

S1 The character in the subject, the action in the verb
When: always, so the sentence names who does what in the words the reader expects to find them in.
Not: when a nominalized action or an abstract subject is the point and the actor is deliberately hidden, which should be rare and marked.
Example: "The clerk lost the file," not "The loss of the file occurred at the clerk's desk."
Misuse: "There was a failure of the system to respond," where nobody is in the subject and nothing is in the verb.

S2 One thing, one name
When: whenever a term has been defined once, since every return pays, and a new name only when the new name is a payment.
Not: when the rename is reached for to avoid repeating a word, which is a rename with no payment.
Example: "The ledger" on page one is "the ledger" on page nine, not "the balance sheet," "the account," and "the tally."
Misuse: a doc that calls the same thing the config, the settings, and the profile, so the reader counts three where there is one.

S3 The common word for the common thing
When: by default, unless the rare word is the only carrier or the common one is loaded by another domain.
Not: when the common word has been claimed by another domain and would open a reading that points away from the ledger.
Example: "He used the key," not "He deployed the implement."
Misuse: "She ambulated to the egress" where "walked to the door" says the same thing and costs the reader nothing.

S4 The narrator's opinion on the word, not in a trailing clause
When: when the narrator has a judgement, put one adverb on the first description, which is shorter, funnier by bathos, and foreshadows.
Not: in a judgement clause after the description, which is the aphorism in another costume and lands in the same place on every page.
Example: "He generously offered to split the bill," not "He offered to split the bill, which was generous of him."
Misuse: "She closed the door, and it was in that small act that one could see the whole of her kindness," the trailing clause explaining what one adverb would have planted.

S5 Open a sentence on its subject
When: by default, or on a phrase the reader cannot take for the subject, so the topic position holds what the reader already has.
Not: on a bare time noun or other phrase that reads as the subject until the real one arrives, since the re-read spends with no raise.
Example: "The train left at six," not "Six o'clock the train left."
Misuse: "Monday mornings fell apart for her," where the reader parses the mornings as the actor and must go back.

S6 One hedge per claim
When: when a claim needs qualifying, give it one hedge, and prefer a number, which pays, to an adjective, which raises.
Not: when hedges stack, since each additional hedge drains trust rather than adding care.
Example: "Most of the forty tests passed," not "A fairly large number of the tests, in most cases, generally seemed to pass."
Misuse: "It could perhaps possibly be argued that the results may somewhat suggest," four hedges and no number.

S7 The example after the claim, the definition before its use
When: when the claim has been stated and an example will pay it, and when a term is used a paragraph after it was defined.
Not: with the example leading and the claim trailing, or a term used before the reader has been given it.
Example: "A raise is a detail that is also a sign. The chipped mug on the mantel is a raise." And "raise" is defined a paragraph before "the ledger" uses it.
Misuse: a paragraph of code before the sentence that says what it shows, and "the pool" used on page two and defined on page five.

S8 Concede before you are forced; the title is the first raise
When: when a counterpoint exists, grant it before the reader reaches it, and treat the title as the first promise the piece must pay.
Not: when the concession is held until a reader or an objection drags it out, or when the title promises what the page never delivers.
Example: "This fails on large files; for anything under a megabyte it is faster," conceded in the sentence that makes the claim, under a title the last line pays.
Misuse: a post titled "Why Everyone Is Wrong About Caching" that never says who, with the obvious counterexample buried in a footnote after a commenter raised it.

S9 A name is a plant at the scale of the cast
When: when two names that bracket a span, or two that share a sound, can say something nothing else on the page points at.
Not: when the narrator glosses the name, since a glossed name is a label and pays nothing.
Example: Sisters named Ada and Ida, and no one on the page remarks on it.
Misuse: "She was called Faith, and faith was the one thing she never had," the name explained the moment it is given.

S10 A cliché is a refrain planted by other texts
When: never as a plant, since it pays nothing; use an exact detail instead, or a phrase borrowed from a character's mouth, which carries the character and the narrator at once.
Not: a borrowed phrase, joke, plot, title or frame, unless the user proposed it, in which case it is their plant and the piece's job is to pay it.
Example: "He left the kettle on the stove with the lid off, the way she had hated," not "He was heartbroken."
Misuse: "It was a dark and stormy night, and time heals all wounds," two refrains planted by other books and paid by none of this one.
