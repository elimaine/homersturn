**F1 The recursive paragraph**
- When: Once in a piece, as its overture or at its end, where each sentence can carry the shared phrase one step further.
- Not: In the middle of a piece, a second time, or when the repeated phrase has nowhere further to go.
- Example: "She kept the key. She kept the key after he left. She kept the key after they changed the locks."
- Misuse: The same opening phrase in paragraph after paragraph, each sentence restating instead of stepping forward.

**F2 The litany**
- When: In a scene that earns it, with exactly as many items as the content has.
- Not: In a post, where it reads as a tic, or when you are padding toward three.
- Example: "He packed the radio, two forks, his mother's letters and one shoe."
- Misuse: Three items by reflex, the third invented for the rhythm.

**F3 The withheld word**
- When: Once, for a word the piece still holds open and will pay, after the reader has reached for it more than once.
- Not: Before the reader has reached for the word, or for a word the piece never pays.
- Example: After two scenes of the town circling what he did, a neighbor says, "Everybody knows you ———."
- Misuse: Blanking a word on first mention to fake mystery, then never paying it.

**F4 The crescendo**
- When: When an evaluation should rise by degrees, starting from a deliberately low first step.
- Not: When the judgement does not change, or when you would start at the top, since cutting the low step kills the climb.
- Example: "The soup was fine, then good, then the best thing he had eaten since the war."
- Misuse: "Extraordinary, astonishing, unbelievable": every step at the top, so nothing climbs.

**F5 The revision**
- When: When the reader holds a planted reading on the matter that the narrator can replace in view, the second clause in the grammar of the first.
- Not: When the first reading was never planted, when it concerns the telling, or when you have already used it twice on the page.
- Example: "I thought he had come to apologize. He had come for his coat."
- Misuse: A "not X, but Y" pivot in every paragraph, correcting readings nobody held.

**F6 The parallel**
- When: Once a piece, to let the reader complete that two characters are one story, with one beat deliberately not mirrored.
- Not: When every beat would mirror, or when the narrator would have to say the likeness aloud.
- Example: Both sisters burn a letter unread, and only the younger, years later, writes back.
- Misuse: A mirror at every beat, which is one character with two names, or a narrator line remarking how alike they are.

**F7 The swear**
- When: On the line that lands, right after a shock the reader just felt, in the mouth of the person it belongs to.
- Not: As seasoning, in the narrator's voice, or anywhere you would follow it with an explanation.
- Example: The surgeon reads the scan, says "Shit," and the room says nothing.
- Misuse: Swearing for color in every scene, then explaining what the word meant.

**F8 The dash (with the semicolon, the comma-and, the participial tail and the parenthesis)**
- When: At most once a paragraph, where a sentence breaks off before the thing arrives, with the parenthesis kept for one under-the-breath aside a page.
- Not: To join two clauses that should be two sentences, and the same goes for the semicolon, the comma-and and the participial tail.
- Example: When the doctor came back, she said only, "It's —"
- Misuse: "The rain fell — the city slept," and every other sentence joined by a dash, a semicolon, an "and" or an "-ing" tail.

**F9 The figure hums too**
- When: On every draft, as a check that keeps each device, verb mood and grammatical person under twice a page and out of consecutive paragraphs, using `--shape` to list repeated frames.
- Not: Against a frame reused on purpose as the refrain, which is the one allowed return.
- Example: Three paragraphs of second-person present, then the fourth drops to first-person past where the speaker's state turns.
- Misuse: One device at a steady rate, such as a rhetorical question closing every paragraph or a single mood and person held across a whole page.

**S1 The character in the subject, the action in the verb**
- When: In every sentence where someone acts, name the actor as the subject and put the act in the verb.
- Not: The skill names no exception, so do not trade it for a noun-heavy passive to sound formal.
- Example: "The board rejected the plan."
- Misuse: "A rejection of the plan was issued by the board," with the actor buried in a "by" phrase and the act turned into a noun.

**S2 One thing, one name**
- When: Always, and especially for a defined term, which pays at every return.
- Not: When the new name is itself a payment, such as "the boy" becoming "the man" in the last line.
- Example: "The ledger" every time, never "the account" or "the tally."
- Misuse: Renaming to avoid repetition, so the server becomes the box, the machine and the host, and readers count four things.

**S3 The common word for the common thing**
- When: By default, for every ordinary object and act.
- Not: When the rare word is the only one that carries the sense, or the common word is loaded by another domain, like "branch" in a git doc.
- Example: "He bought a car."
- Misuse: "He acquired an automobile," a thesaurus swap of "utilize" for "use" and "commence" for "start."

**S4 The narrator's opinion on the word, not in a trailing clause**
- When: When the narrator judges something, put one adverb or adjective on the first description.
- Not: When the opinion is on the telling instead of the matter, since that is the narrator serving the narrator.
- Example: "His mercifully brief speech began at nine."
- Misuse: "His speech began at nine, which, frankly, was more than anyone needed," a trailing judgement that works as an aphorism in costume.

**S5 Open a sentence on its subject**
- When: By default, open on the subject so the reader knows at once what the sentence is about.
- Not: The rule is not needed when the opener is a phrase nobody could mistake for a subject, such as "On Saturday night."
- Example: "The band played until two on Saturday night."
- Misuse: "Saturday Night the band played until two," where a bare time noun first reads as the band's name and forces a re-read.

**S6 One hedge per claim**
- When: When a claim is uncertain, give it one hedge, or better, a number that carries the uncertainty.
- Not: When a number is available, use it instead of an adjective, because a number pays and an adjective only raises.
- Example: "The fix probably cuts load time to 1.2 seconds."
- Misuse: "This may possibly somewhat improve performance in some cases," stacked hedges that drain the reader's trust.

**S7 The example after the claim, the definition before its use**
- When: State the claim, then show it, and define a term a paragraph before it first does work.
- Not: Do not lead with the example before the reader knows what it proves, or define a term after it has been used.
- Example: "The opening is too dense: its first sentence runs sixty words across four clauses."
- Misuse: A paragraph of anecdote with the point arriving last, or a term used three times before its definition.

**S8 Concede before you are forced; the title is the first raise**
- When: When an objection the reader will hold is coming, concede it early, and write a title that raises what the piece will pay.
- Not: For an objection no reader holds, since conceding it is the denied reading nobody held.
- Example: A post titled "Why We Still Ship on Fridays" that names the outage risk in its second paragraph.
- Misuse: Conceding only after a reply forces it, or a title that raises a claim the piece never pays.

**S9 A name is a plant at the scale of the cast**
- When: For an important character, choose a name that opens a reading before they speak, then leave it alone.
- Not: For a walk-on, who gets a noun, or when the narrator would gloss or point at the name.
- Example: A loan officer named Mercy who never shows any, and nobody on the page remarks on it.
- Misuse: "Her name was Grace, and it suited her," a glossed name that becomes a label.

**S10 A cliché is a refrain planted by other texts**
- When: When a stock phrase arrives, replace it with an exact detail or a phrase taken from a character's mouth.
- Not: For a borrowed joke, plot or frame the user proposed, which is their plant for the piece to pay.
- Example: Not "her heart sank" but "she put the phone face down and kept it there."
- Misuse: "At the end of the day" in the narrator's voice, a line lifted from the skill's own examples, or an unrequested borrowed plot twist.
