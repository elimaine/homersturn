F1 The recursive paragraph
When: Once a piece, at the end or as the overture, when one phrase can be carried a step further by each sentence until it arrives somewhere.
Not: When the phrase has nowhere to go, or when the piece already used the figure elsewhere, since once is the limit.
Example: "She kept the key. She kept the key in the drawer. She kept the key in the drawer where he had left it. She kept the key in the drawer where he had left it the week he said he would come back."
Misuse: Every paragraph opens on its own repeated phrase and the steps add words without moving, so the device is a frame reused at the same density.

F2 The litany
When: In a scene that has earned it, when a list said as prose has exactly as many items as the content has.
Not: In a post, where it reads as a tic, or whenever the count is three because three felt right.
Example: "On the table were the bill, the ring, the unopened letter from the bank, and the one cup he had washed."
Misuse: A list of three vague nouns by reflex, in every third paragraph, with nothing in the scene that asked to be counted.

F3 The withheld word
When: Once, when the piece still holds a word open and will pay it, and only after the reader has reached for it more than once.
Not: Before the reader has reached for the word at all, or when the piece never pays it, or a second time on the same page.
Example: After two scenes where no one says what was in the river, the father finally starts, "It was my ———," and the chapter ends.
Misuse: Three dashes dropped in for suspense on a word the reader never wanted, or one the next line explains, so the withholding spends and pays nothing.

F4 The crescendo
When: When the evaluative word can climb by degrees across the beat and the low first step is kept so the climb is heard.
Not: When there is only one degree to say, or when the first step is cut because it looked weak, since then the climb is gone.
Example: "The room was cold. The room was cold enough to see breath. The room was cold enough that the dog would not come in."
Misuse: Opening on the strongest word and then repeating it in synonyms, so the line only hums louder instead of rising.

F5 The revision
When: Twice a page at most, when the reader holds the same reading the narrator does and the second clause can replace it in the grammar of the first, so the reader revises with the narrator.
Not: When the first reading was never planted, when the correction is about the telling rather than the matter, or in every paragraph, where the pivot is the figure humming.
Example: "He had come to apologize, she decided from the flowers, until she saw they were the ones from her own garden."
Misuse: "He was angry—no, not angry, something else," correcting a reading nobody held, with a dash and a "no" doing the work the second clause should have.

F6 The parallel
When: One per piece, when two characters can be given the same beat in different keys and one beat deliberately not mirrored, so the second pays the first where the arcs meet.
Not: When every beat mirrors, which makes one character with two names, or when the narrator would have to name the pairing or carry it by a line instead of beats.
Example: Both brothers are offered the farm on the same porch; the elder asks the price, the younger asks who else was asked, and nothing else about the scene is said.
Misuse: The narrator writes "like his brother before him" at each matched beat, and the two characters answer every situation the same way.

F7 The swear
When: One hit on the line that lands, earned by a shock the reader just felt, in the mouth of the person it belongs to, followed by silence.
Not: In the narrator's voice, before the shock, as a running register, or with any explanation after it.
Example: She opens the envelope, reads the name on the deed, and says, "Shit." The next line is a different day.
Misuse: A character swears in every scene for flavor, and the narrator adds that the word showed how much it hurt.

F8 The dash (with the semicolon, the comma-and, the participial tail and the parenthesis)
When: At most one dash a paragraph, only where a sentence breaks off before the thing arrives, and the parenthesis once a page as the aside under the breath.
Not: To join two clauses that should have been two sentences, which the semicolon, the comma-and and the participial tail do the same way and hum the same way.
Example: "He reached for the light, and then the door—"
Misuse: "The town was quiet—the kind of quiet that means something; everyone had gone inside, waiting (as they always did)."

F9 The figure hums too
When: When checking a page for one device at a steady rate, since each figure may appear at most twice a page and never in consecutive paragraphs, and mood and person show a state first.
Not: When the repeated frame is the refrain, which is the one frame that may return, or when the rhythm itself is the content.
Example: A page where one paragraph is a litany, the next plain, a later one a revision, and the verb mood shifts to the conditional only where the speaker's state turns to doubt.
Misuse: Every paragraph opens on a participial phrase in the second person imperative and ends on a dash, at one steady rate, so the reader stops hearing any of it.

S1 The character in the subject, the action in the verb
When: Whenever a sentence has an agent and something they do, so the reader finds the actor at the top and the act in the verb.
Not: When the thing that acts is not a character, or when the one carrier of the sense is a nominal the reader needs as the topic.
Example: "The clerk lost the file," not "The loss of the file occurred at the clerk's desk."
Misuse: "There was a decision on the part of the committee regarding the closure," where the committee hides and the verb is "was."

S2 One thing, one name
When: Always, unless the new name is a payment; a term defined once and used throughout is a raise that pays at every return.
Not: When a term is used once, which is a rename, or when the thing is renamed only to avoid repeating a word.
Example: Call it "the ledger" in paragraph one and "the ledger" in paragraph nine, never "the balance sheet" in between.
Misuse: "The pool," then "the reservoir," then "the reader's budget," for the same thing, so the reader spends on reconciling names.

S3 The common word for the common thing
When: Whenever the common word carries the sense and no other domain has loaded it.
Not: When the rare word is the only carrier, or when the common one is loaded by another domain and would open a reading that points away from the ledger.
Example: "He left," not "He absented himself."
Misuse: Reaching for "liminal" where "between" was right, or using "load" in a page about freight where the reader will take it as electrical.

S4 The narrator's opinion on the word, not in a trailing clause
When: When the narrator judges, put one adverb on the first description, which is shorter, funnier by bathos, and foreshadows.
Not: Never as a judgement clause after the description, which is the aphorism in another costume and lands in the same place on every page.
Example: "He generously poured himself the last of the wine."
Misuse: "He poured himself the last of the wine, which told you everything about the kind of man he was."

S5 Open a sentence on its subject
When: Whenever the first words can be the subject, or a phrase the reader cannot mistake for one.
Not: Not on a bare time noun or other phrase that reads as the subject until the real one arrives, since the re-read is a spend with no raise.
Example: "The train left at noon," not "Noon the train left."
Misuse: "Tuesday morning the board, having met twice already, voted," where the reader takes Tuesday as the actor and has to go back.

S6 One hedge per claim
When: When a claim needs a hedge, give it one, and prefer a number, which pays, over an adjective, which raises.
Not: Not stacked hedges on one claim, which drain trust, and not where a number is available and an adjective is used instead.
Example: "The test passed 94 times out of 100," not "The test seems to mostly pass fairly reliably."
Misuse: "It might perhaps be somewhat possible that this could arguably help," five hedges on one claim.

S7 The example after the claim, the definition before its use
When: When a claim needs an example, put the example after it; when a term will be used, define it a paragraph before its use.
Not: Not the example before the claim it illustrates, and not a term used first and defined after, since the reader is lost at its first appearance.
Example: "A raise is a detail that is also a sign. A gun on the mantel is a raise." Then the next paragraph uses "raise" freely.
Misuse: A page that opens on "the stress position" in sentence two and explains what it is on page three.

S8 Concede before you are forced; the title is the first raise
When: Concede the objection the reader will have before they raise it, and make the title the first promise the piece will pay.
Not: Not a concession the reader could not have made, and not a title that raises what the piece never pays.
Example: An essay titled "Why the Rule Fails" that opens, "The rule works for short pieces, and it works for most of them."
Misuse: A title like "The Only Way to Write" over a piece that defends the claim only after every objection has been forced on it.

S9 A name is a plant at the scale of the cast
When: When a name can open a reading before the person has spoken and nothing on the page points at it, so the reader never notices they were told.
Not: Not when the narrator glosses the name, which turns the plant into a label, and not when the page points at what the name means.
Example: A landlord named Mr. Grice who, four scenes later, keeps the deposit.
Misuse: "His name was Mr. Grice, and like his name he was greedy."

S10 A cliché is a refrain planted by other texts
When: Replace it with an exact detail, which pays in kind, or a phrase borrowed from a character's mouth, which carries the character and the narrator at once.
Not: Never as a line to use, including a borrowed joke, plot, title or frame, unless the user proposed it, in which case it is their plant and the piece's job is to pay it.
Example: Not "her heart was in her throat" but "she counted the stairs twice and got different numbers."
Misuse: "At the end of the day, it was a rollercoaster of emotions," plus a plot lifted from a film the user never mentioned.
