## Piece

Ruth put the keys on the bench instead of in Mae's hand. "The oven runs hot on the left," she said. "Load the left last."

"You told me." Mae did not look up from the scale.

"I'm telling you again." Ruth had her coat on and her hand on the door, and had had both for some minutes. "I'll be asleep by one."

Mae said, "Go," and Ruth went.

The door shut. The proof box hummed. Behind Mae were four hundred rolls that did not exist yet, and between her and six o'clock were her own two hands. She scaled. She shaped. She loaded the left first, because she forgot, and burned the whole tray black. She opened the back door to let the smoke out and did not cry about it, much.

Forty years before, Walter had put the keys on this bench. He had said the oven ran hot on the left, and that he would be asleep by one, and Ruth had burned her first tray too, and at four, carrying the bins out, had found his truck at the curb with the lights off and Walter asleep at the wheel. He never said he had been there. She never said she had looked.

At four Mae carried the bins out. The alley was empty. The street was empty. A cat, the streetlight, nobody's truck. She stood a while in the cold with her apron on, longer than the cold was worth. Then she went in, because the second proof was up and nobody else was going to load it.

Across town Ruth sat at her kitchen table in her coat, with the car keys in front of her, and had not been asleep by one. At four she took the coat off.
