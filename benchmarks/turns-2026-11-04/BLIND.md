# Turns bench, blind sheet

Two pieces per brief, lettered P and Q at random. Rank each pair: which is the better piece for its brief, and in a few words why. The key is sealed in `blind-key.json`.

## biopsy

*Brief:* A scene, 300 to 400 words, close third person. A woman sits at her kitchen table waiting for the clinic to call about a biopsy. The call comes and the news is benign. On her way out to the garden afterwards she finds the cat dead on the porch step. Plain meaning: the result is good and the cat is dead. What it carries: relief does not get to stay. Ambiguity 4, reader strength 3. The mindset is yours to set. It must move at least twice, each move with a cause on the page, and end in a state it did not start in.

### biopsy P

The phone lay face up on the kitchen table. She had turned it so the screen faced her and then turned it so it did not. Ten forty. They had said between ten and twelve. Through the window the cat was on the porch step in the sun. He had been there when she sat down and he had stayed.

She could hear the fridge, and the clock in the hall, and a car two streets over that did not come closer. She had decided last night what she would do after, either way. Either way she would go out and get the bulbs in; she had said it to Dan on the phone and then again to herself, with the trowel already by the back door. The sun had gone off the step and the cat had stayed.

When it rang she let it ring twice, because answering on the first would have been something, and then she answered. A nurse, not the doctor, which meant— she could not finish what it meant before the nurse had said her name and the date and the word. Benign. She asked her to say it again and the nurse did, kindly, as if people asked. There was a letter coming. There was nothing else they needed from her. She said thank you three times and heard herself do it and could not stop, and when the line went she sat with the phone in her hand and the kitchen was exactly the kitchen, the fridge, the clock, the car gone.

She took the trowel and the bulbs and opened the back door. The cat was on the step with his back to her, the way he lay when he wanted the sun, except the sun was over on the fence now and he had not followed it. She said his name. She put the bag down and bent and put her hand on him, and the fur was warm where it faced the house and not warm underneath, and his eye was open on the step, and there was a fly.

She sat down beside him on the step and stayed.

### biopsy Q

She had the phone face down on the table, which changed nothing, since it would ring either way. Nine, the clinic had said. It was ten to. The cat's bowl by the back door was still full from last night, the biscuits gone soft, and the cat had not come for it, and she had not gone to the door to call, because the door was for after. If it was good she would do the beds. She had said this to nobody, to the kitchen, more than once. Sun lay across the table in a strip and she kept her hands out of it.

Nine came and did nothing. Nine past. Nine twelve. She turned the phone over so she could watch it not ring, which was worse, and turned it back.

It rang at nineteen past and she had it to her ear before the second ring. A woman's voice, pleasant, reading from something. Benign. The woman said it twice, the second time slower, as if it were a word people needed to hear slowly, then something about a letter, and nothing further. She said thank you. She said thank you again after the line had gone. She was standing, and could not have said when she had stood, and the strip of sun was on her hands and she let it stay. The cup, the chair pushed back, the clock at twenty past, all of it ordinary and hers.

The garden, then. She had said. She took the spade from behind the door, because the beds wanted turning and a thing said to a kitchen is still said, and went past the full bowl and opened the back door onto the step.

The cat was on the step. On its side in the sun, flies at its mouth, in the place it lay on any afternoon. She stood with the door open and the spade in her hand. She crouched and put her hand on its side. It was warm. That was the sun. She had the spade already.

## tools

*Brief:* A scene, 350 to 450 words. Two brothers divide their dead father's tools in his garage. The elder keeps every fact in order and stays civil until the younger picks up the wooden plane their father never let either of them use. Plain meaning: they split the tools. What it carries: who the father let near what. Ambiguity 4, reader strength 3. The elder's restraint must fail once on the page and come back, and the scene must end in a different state from the one it opened in. Both brothers need an entrance.

### tools P

Peter had the chisels out before Danny got there, the six Marples in their roll on the bench and the four loose Stanleys beside them, oldest at the left. "The Stanleys were his first," he said. "Nineteen seventy-one, from the Woolworths in Bolton. Either set is yours."

Danny came in with the coffee he'd been sent for and set both cups on the bandsaw table. Peter moved them to the floor. "I'll have whichever one you weren't going to tell me about," Danny said, and took the Stanleys without looking at them.

They went down the wall. The tenon saw, the panel saw, the ripsaw with the missing tooth, Peter said whose each had been and where it had come from and Danny said right, and the drill was the one from the house in Farnworth and Danny said right, and the router had never been out of its box and Danny said right and put it on his side. Peter put the chisels on his.

Above the bench was the plane. Beech, dark at the handle where a hand went, the iron ground by their father and by his father before that, the one thing in the garage neither boy had been let touch, and Danny reached up and took it down and turned it over and ran his thumb along the sole, the way you check for a nick.

"Put that down."

Danny didn't.

"That isn't on the list. That isn't yours or mine. He never let me near it. He never let you near it, he never let anyone, put it down, put it fucking down."

Danny put it down.

Peter picked up a screwdriver and said what it was. Then another. He got to the end of the drawer and the fact of each one came out level and he did not look at the shelf.

"He let me," Danny said. "October. He couldn't close his hand on it by then, so he had me do the last pass on the drawer front while he watched. He said I'd got the angle."

Peter had been in Leeds in October. He had the dates for that too.

He picked the plane up off the bench. "Right," he said, and wrote it on Danny's side.

### tools Q

Martin had the sockets sorted before Tommy's car was in the drive. Two sets, one metric, one not. The metric set was from '88, the week the Chevy went. He wrote it on the clipboard and looked once at the shelf over the bench, where the wooden plane had sat for thirty years under the one rule the garage ever had. Not that one.

Tommy came in by the side door the way he had always come in, the door first and him after it. "You brought a clipboard," he said. "To Dad's garage. A clipboard, Mart."

"Two drills," Martin said. "The corded one's older. Better motor. Yours."

"Okay." Tommy took it the way he took things, without looking at it.

The hand tools went fast. Hammers, two, and Tommy's was the one with the taped handle, since he was the one who had taped it. The saws he split by use, the crosscut to Tommy, who would cut with it, the dovetail to Martin, who would keep it. The flashlight on the hook by the door was not on the list. It had been Martin's job, winters, to stand where he was put and hold it on the work, and it was nobody's tool. Tommy reached for the cordless. "Not that one," Martin said. "I bought him that." Tommy's hand came off it fast.

Tommy was tall enough. He had been tall enough since he was fourteen, and he reached up and took the plane off the shelf as if it were a tool, and turned it over, and ran a thumb down the sole.

"Not that one," Martin said, and it came out in his father's voice, and then it was his own. "Put it down. You never once—he'd have let you. He'd have fucking let you."

The compressor ticked. Tommy set the plane on the bench between them, iron up, and took his hand away.

"Levels," Martin said. "Three. The four-foot's yours. You've got the fence to do." He wrote it down. Tommy slid the plane across the bench until it touched the clipboard. Martin wrote that down too. Then he picked up the plane.

## backlog

*Brief:* A post, 250 to 350 words, for a general reader who can leave at every scroll, arguing that a software team should delete its whole backlog once a year. Plain meaning: delete it. What it carries: what a backlog is for the people who keep it. Halfway through, concede the strongest objection honestly and let the concession change the writer's state before the claim comes back. The end is a consequence, never an aphorism. Ambiguity 3, reader strength 3.

### backlog P

**Delete the backlog**

Once a year, delete the backlog. Not archive it, not move it to a board named Someday. Delete it, every ticket below the first screen.

Open yours and read from the bottom. The oldest tickets are three years old, and each was urgent once. Somebody wrote it on a bad Thursday with a customer on the phone, and writing it was how they put the thing down. Nobody has opened it since. Nobody will, because the work the team does this month comes from this month's bad Thursday, and the list is only where the old ones went.

The objection is the right one, and I would make it myself. The backlog is memory. Somewhere in the bottom hundred is one real bug, reported with a screenshot and a date, and if you delete the list you delete that too, and in spring you meet it again as a stranger. That is true. You will lose things, and some of them were real, and the person who reported the real one will remember that they did.

But look at what they did when they reported it. They wrote it down so they could put it down. The ticket was never a plan. It was a receipt, proof the thing had been seen, and once it was filed somebody could go home. That is what the list is for the people who keep it. It holds the things they could not do and could not say no to, and it holds them so that they do not have to.

So delete it, and say why at the standup, in those words. Nothing in the bottom hundred was ever going to be done by a list, and the people who put it there know that better than you do. In February the real bug comes back, filed again by the same person with the same screenshot, and this time it is near the top, and this time somebody reads it.

### backlog Q

Once a year, delete the backlog. Not the stale half, and not into a column called Someday. Delete it.

A backlog is the list of things a team has agreed not to do yet. Open the oldest ticket in yours. Somebody wrote it with care. Nobody has touched it since, and nobody will, and everyone who has scrolled past it knows that. It stays because closing it would mean telling the person who wrote it, and the team would rather let the list do the telling slowly.

The objection is the right one, so here it is in full. You will lose things that mattered. The bug a user reproduced step by step. The cleanup somebody scoped and never got a sprint for. Those were real, and they were given, and deleting them throws away work by people who did it for free. I have no answer to that. Some of it will not come back.

Then why do it. Because of what the list was for the people who kept it. It was never a plan. It was the place a team put a no it did not want to say, so that nobody had to say it, and the person who asked could go on being told not yet. Keeping that careful bug report there was not keeping faith with its author. It was keeping them waiting.

Delete it, then, and see what the first week brings. The things that mattered come back, filed again by somebody who still wants them. The things that did not, do not. And the no the list was saying slowly, somebody now has to say out loud, to the person who asked.

## hatch

*Brief:* A game turn, 150 to 250 words, second person present tense. The player has just forced open a sealed hatch in the floor of a derelict station's galley; below it is dark and warm. The turn must pay a fact, a cost, a reaction, a threat and a route, and keep the player's intent. The narrator's state must climb from curiosity through fear to something larger by the last line, each step caused by what the player perceives. Ambiguity 3, reader strength 3.

### hatch P

The hatch comes up on the third heave, and the bar comes up bent. Warm air climbs past your face. There is something in it you want to call bread and can't. The seam that held the hatch down was welded from underneath.

The hum stops. You have had it in your boots since the airlock and took it for the air plant. It stops the way a voice stops when a door opens. Below you, in the dark, something takes a breath and does not let it out. The hatch lies on its back against the galley wall, its hinge in two pieces on the deck, your doing, and it will not go down again. The first rung of the ladder is warm.

You lean your light into the shaft. It goes down past the next deck, past where the hull should be, past any deck the station has, and far under all of that something glows and dims and glows, slower than you breathe. The corridor is behind you, lit. The ladder is under your hand. Below, the breath it was holding comes out, warm, up past your face, and the light comes up with it, and every lamp in the galley dims to make room.

### hatch Q

You get the bar under the lip and the hatch comes up with a sound like a jar opening. Warm air climbs out of it and over your hands. The manifest listed this station cold for eleven years, and nothing stays warm on its own for eleven years. The bar is bent where you leaned on it. You will not get it under anything again.

The warm has a rhythm in it. In, and out. Something down there is pulling the air and letting it go, and on every out the galley strips over your head brighten, and on every in they dim. The station is on its breath. Rust, and under the rust something sweet. Your boots are on the rim and the dark is a hand's width from them. On the next out it rises — not air this time, the dark itself, come up to the lip to see what let the warm out.

You put the torch down the shaft. The ladder goes down. It goes past the second deck, and past where the station has a third, and the beam gives out before the rungs do. What you have been walking on for a day is not a station. It is a lid. The galley door is behind you and open. The ladder is in front of you and warm. It breathes in again. Small, on the lip of it.

## house

*Brief:* A reflection, 250 to 350 words, first person, on the morning the writer's childhood house is handed to its buyers. Plain meaning: the keys change hands. What it carries: what was already gone before the sale. One sentence returns verbatim three times, and its sense must change each time because the state behind it has changed, not because a new fact arrived. Ambiguity 4, reader strength 4.

### house P

The keys were on the counter where the agent had said to leave them, four on a ring with no fob. The movers had come on Tuesday. I walked through every room once, to be sure. The hall light was still wired the wrong way, down for on. There was nothing left to take.

The buyers were early. A couple, younger than I had pictured, a car seat in the back. She asked about the hall light and I showed her, down for on, and she laughed, and I heard how the laugh would carry in that hall for years without me. They went to measure the back bedroom. I stayed in the kitchen, because the kitchen was where I had expected it.

I stood where the table had been and waited. The light came in at the angle that used to make my mother squint at the paper. Whatever I had come for did not come. It was not in the room. Standing there I knew it had not been in the room for a long time, longer than the sale, longer than the boxes, and that I had kept it somewhere else and called it the house. There was nothing left to take.

They came out of the back bedroom with the tape still open. I gave her the keys. She dropped them in her coat pocket without looking, the way you put away something that is already yours. I went down the steps. The morning had warmed while we were inside. I sat in the car with nowhere to be until evening. There was nothing left to take.

### house Q

**Completion**

The buyers were early, and so was I. We stood on the step while the agent found the right page, and I took the keys out of my coat. Three on a ring: front, back, the shed. My mother had put them in my hand a year ago and I had kept them in the drawer with the others, and the back door one still stuck. I said so. They said they'd get it looked at. It was only the keys.

I walked them through. The hall light is on the wrong switch. The third stair takes your weight on the left. The boiler wants the button held, not pressed. I knew all of it in the voice you use for directions, and they nodded the way people nod at directions, which is to say they would find it out again on their own. Nobody had needed me to know it for some time.

I had thought of the year as waiting. The drive over on Sundays, the taps run so the pipes would not sulk, the post off the mat, the heating on an hour and off. It was not waiting. Waiting is for someone who is coming. She had put the keys in my hand as you hand over a thing you have finished with, and I had taken them as you take one. It was only the keys.

They went in. I went down the path to the gate and did not look back, because what the window had in it now was two people I had met an hour ago, finding the switch.

In the car my hand went to my coat for them and found the lining. That was the whole of the change. I had known that house by its faults for longer than I had lived in it, and I had carried three keys about for a year as if a ring of metal could keep a house. It was only the keys.

## friend

*Brief:* A message, 150 to 220 words, to a friend the writer has not spoken to in six years after a falling-out that was the writer's fault, asking nothing except whether they would meet. The reader has no reason to finish. Plain meaning: I was wrong, will you meet me. What it carries: the writer knows what it cost the other person. It starts in shame and must not end there, and the move out of shame must be earned on the page, not announced. Ambiguity 2, reader strength 3.

### friend P

It's Mara. I don't know if this is still your number. I was wrong, six years ago, and I have known it the whole time. I'm writing to ask whether you'd meet me, and for nothing else.

I've started this more times than I'll say. Every version was about me. How sorry I am, how much I've thought about it, how I'm different now. You'd have read two lines and put the phone down, and you'd have been right.

What I kept leaving out was you. You stopped coming to Jo's on Thursdays because I'd be there. You missed her wedding because I'd be there. You gave up a room full of people rather than sit across from one, and I let you, for six years, and I kept my seat.

I've given the seat up. Jo knows why, and the rest will if they ask. I'm not telling you so you'll come. I'm telling you so you can go back without wondering. Either way, the room is yours.

### friend Q

It was my fault. You knew that before I did, and you've known it for six years, which is a long time to know something alone.

I've started this message before and stopped at the same place every time, the place where I'd explain. Every explanation I have makes it smaller, and it wasn't small for you. So I'm not going to explain.

You never said a word against me. I know because I spent the first year listening for one, so I could call myself right. You said nothing, so people read your silence as an admission, and you let them, and some of them went. I said plenty. I don't know how many of them you got back.

I'm not asking you to tell me. I'm not asking for anything you'd have to write down. Would you meet me? You pick the place and the hour and how long, and you leave when it's enough. This time I'll do the waiting.
