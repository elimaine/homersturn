## Piece

The sword was stuck in the floor of the pantry, between two sacks of flour, and Pip found it because she was the only one small enough to go looking down there.

It was too big. It was taller than she was and twice as heavy, and when she pulled it free the point dragged a line in the dust all the way to the door. "Well," she said, to no one, "someone has to carry it." She did not say she was brave. Brave mice say so; she only wedged it under her arm and went up the stairs.

Up the stairs was the kingdom, and the kingdom was afraid.

It was afraid of Lord Dustvelvet, who had come in the autumn and never left, who sat on the warm hearthstone that used to be everyone's and licked his enormous grey paws clean after each meal. "You may all keep working," he would purr, settling deeper into the ash. "I find I rule best after lunch." His fur was the softest thing in the house. The mice who groomed it were never seen again.

I called him cruel, at first, the way you call winter cruel—no: winter takes what it needs and goes, and he took and took and called it appetite, which is worse than cruelty, because cruelty at least knows it is doing wrong.

So when Pip came up the stairs dragging a sword as long as a sentence, the kingdom did not cheer. They told her to put it back. They told her he was soft, he was comfortable, he would leave in spring. They told her the sword was too big for her.

"I know," she said.

She crossed the floor. Dustvelvet opened one eye, then the other, and for the first time in a year he was looking up at something instead of down.

The fight was not long and it was not pretty. A mouse with a sword taller than herself does not fence; she falls forward in the right direction and holds on. She was knocked down three times. Three times the sword was heavier than she was, and three times she picked it up, because the only thing worse than lifting it was what happened if she didn't.

When it was over the hearthstone was empty, and warm, and it was everyone's again.

They wanted to make her a statue. They wanted to put the sword over the mantel where the children could see it. Pip wedged it back under her arm.

"Someone has to carry it," she said. "In case."

And the mice who had told her it was too big for her said nothing, and the youngest of them, who had watched the whole thing from the top of the stairs, went down into the pantry to see what else was small enough for only her to find.
