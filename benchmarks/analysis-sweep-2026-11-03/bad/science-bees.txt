Honeybee colonies choose new nest sites through a remarkable process of collective decision-making, usually led by scout bees. When a colony outgrows its hive, roughly half of the workers and the old queen leave in a swarm and cluster temporarily on a branch or other sheltered spot. Meanwhile, about 5 to 10 percent of the worker bees act as scouts, flying out in search of suitable cavities.

Scouts evaluate potential sites based on several key features: volume (ideally around 40 liters), a small entrance (which is easier to defend), a height above the ground, and protection from weather and predators. When a scout finds a promising location, she assesses it and returns to the swarm to perform a waggle dance. The intensity and duration of her dance reflect her assessment of the site's quality, and the direction and length of the waggle run indicate where it lies.

Other scouts may then visit the advertised sites and, if they agree, dance for them as well. Over time, a site that attracts more support gains momentum, while weaker options fade as their dances lose energy. Scouts also tend to abandon sites that are poor, which helps prevent the swarm from getting stuck on a bad choice.

This process continues until a quorum is reached. Once enough scouts have visited and agreed on a single site, the swarm rapidly lifts off and flies directly to the new home. Researchers such as Thomas Seeley have shown that this decentralized method, which relies on competition, feedback, and a threshold of agreement, often produces remarkably good choices without any single bee being in charge.
