The storm had outrun the forecast, and by the time Maren reached the lighthouse, rain was driving sideways across the rocks like thrown gravel. She shouldered the door shut and leaned against it, water pooling at her boots.

The lamp room waited above her, its beam sweeping the black water every twelve seconds. She climbed the iron spiral stairs with one hand on the cold rail, counting steps the way her father had taught her. Forty-one. Forty-two. At the top, the glass shuddered in its frame and the wind found every seam.

She sat on the keeper's stool and watched the light turn. Below, the sea hurled itself against the tower with a sound like doors slamming in an empty house.

Her phone had no signal. The radio had died an hour ago, swallowed by static. So she waited, as her father had waited here for thirty winters, listening for the moment the wind would drop or the tower would crack, whichever came first.

Near midnight, the beam caught something: a dark hull, low in the swell, too far out to be anything but lost. Maren stood and pressed her palm to the glass, counting the seconds until the light came round again. There. Still there. Smaller now, or maybe just farther.

She lifted the flare gun from the locker and checked the cartridge the way he'd shown her. Waiting out a storm, she understood, was not the same as being safe. Some nights the light was the only thing you could do.
