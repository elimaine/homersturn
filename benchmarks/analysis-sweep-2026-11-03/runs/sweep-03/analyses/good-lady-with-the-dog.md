# Analysis

## 1. Purpose and mindset

**Purpose: a scene, a story.** The evidence is third-person past tense, a named man in a named town, a quoted thought, and backstory delivered as summary. The capitalised "IT" signals a first page. The genre's default dial is 4 to 5. This piece runs at about 3 to 4. Its readings are held across paragraphs (who she is, whether she is alone), and second senses sit under single lines (the irony on the wife). Nothing is withheld past the excerpt that the excerpt promised to pay.

Rules that turn with the genre:
- The reader chose to stay, so several open loops are allowed.
- The point belongs at the bottom, not the top.
- Withholding is permitted.
- Summary is the right tempo for backstory.

**Mindset: composed, dry, amused distance, 2 of 5.** The evidence:
- **Long joined sentences** that never break.
- **No accents spent:** no swear and no punch line.
- **Hedges throughout:** "probably," "seemed," "almost always," "It seemed to him."
- **Judgement delivered in other people's words:** "as she said of herself," "the lower race."
- **One thing walked around:** "some force." Everything else is named.

No mindset was given, so this is the help mode. Here is the same line in three states, for you to pick by ear. The original: "he secretly considered her unintelligent, narrow, inelegant, was afraid of her, and did not like to be at home."

- **Cold contempt, 4:** "He thought her narrow in private, feared her in person, and kept away from home."
- **Rueful, 3:** "He thought her narrow and inelegant, and he was afraid of her, and home was the place he liked least."
- **Stripped, 5:** "He thought her narrow. He was afraid of her. He did not like to be at home."

- **What a reader sees before reading:**
  - **A first page.** The capitalised "IT" says so.
  - **An opening at the edge of what the eye takes whole.** It is three sentences, within the rule, but 74 words.
  - **A shrinking run.** The first three paragraphs get shorter, down to a one-line thought.
  - **Then two walls.** That is the pattern the skill marks: a one-line paragraph followed by a block the eye cannot take. Because the reader chose to stay, the walls cost less here than they would in a post.

- **Raised and never paid, within the excerpt:** who she is, whether she is alone, the "force." All three are legitimate for an opening that keeps going.
- **Paid and never raised:** nothing. The backstory looks unprompted, but ¶3's "without a husband" raised it.

## 4. The seams

- **¶1 → ¶2: "and then," literally.** "And afterwards he met her…" There is no cause and no turn. By the skill's rule this paragraph is a list. (See the final section for why it works anyway.)
- **¶2 → ¶3: therefore.** The repeated word carries the link: "walking alone" becomes "here alone."
- **¶3 → ¶4: but.** He checks her for a husband while he has a wife. No connective is used; the turn sits in "married" answering "husband." This is the best seam in the piece.
- **¶4 → ¶5: but, with the repeated noun.** ¶4 ends on "the lower race" and ¶5 brings it back to close its first sentence. He despises women, and he cannot last two days without them.

Tempo by paragraph:
- **¶1:** summary into a glimpse.
- **¶2:** summary, iterative ("several times a day").
- **¶3:** pause, on one thought.
- **¶4:** summary covering years.
- **¶5:** summary of habit.

Every paragraph is in the right tempo. There is no scene tempo spent on summary material.

## 6. The topic position

The known-new chain holds throughout:
- **¶4** opens on "He," the man whose thought we just read.
- **¶5** opens on "It seemed to him … call them," where "them" reaches back across the break to "women."
- **One place to check:** ¶5's second sentence, "with them he was cold," where "them" is men, not the women of the sentence before. It resolves on one read.

## 7. Accidental readings

- **Scope of a modifier.** "he secretly considered her unintelligent, narrow, inelegant, was afraid of her, and did not like to be at home". Does "secretly" govern the fear too? Keep it open; the ambiguity pays.
- **Two hedges on one claim.** "probably on that account, almost always spoke ill of women". Keep "almost," because it carries the claim. "Probably" is the narrator's irony, so cut nothing. Note it only.
- **A thing renamed.** The woman is "a new person," then "a lady with a little dog," then "a fair-haired young lady," then "the lady with the dog." The dog is renamed three times. By the rule, close it. In practice, don't (see the final section).
- **A modifier before its noun.** "he saw, walking on the sea-front, a fair-haired young lady" could briefly read as him walking. "Sitting" rules that out. No fix needed.
- **No pointing participles and no noun stacks.**

## 8. Unearned weight

- **A colon reveal.** "IT was said that a new person had appeared on the sea-front: a lady with a little dog." By the rule it promises weight and pays an ordinary line. Here the ordinary line is the point (see the final section).
- **Three by reflex, almost.**
  - "In his appearance, in his character, in his whole nature" is a litany in the narrator's voice. It is near reflex but widens with each item. The fix would be two items, or keep it.
  - "unintelligent, narrow, inelegant" is not reflex. It is a triad that breaks into a fourth item, "was afraid of her," which undoes the first three.
- **The denied-reading form, used legitimately.** "not Dmitri, but Dimitri" has the banned shape, but the denied reading is real: it is his name. No fix.
- **A dash.** "long ago--had been unfaithful to her often". The sentence breaks off and restarts harder. That is the redeemed use, and it is the only dash in the paragraph. Keep it.
- **An abstraction in the subject slot.** "there was something attractive and elusive which allured women" hides who acts. Fix: "women were drawn by something in him." By contrast, "some force seemed to draw him" should keep its abstraction, because the agent is the thing being withheld.
- **No importance claims, recaps, swears or fake kickers.**

## 9. The three changes that would pay the most

These pay little. The piece already does what its genre asks.

1. **Wall after a one-liner.** ¶4 runs 140 words directly after the 20-word ¶3. Fix: break after "did not like to be at home," so that the infidelity sentence stands alone and lands "the lower race" just before ¶5 picks it up.
2. **Stress spent on a tag.** In ¶3, "…to make her acquaintance," Gurov reflected. Fix: move the tag to the front so the paragraph ends on "acquaintance."
3. **Abstraction as subject.** "there was something attractive and elusive which allured women." Fix: "women were drawn by something attractive and elusive in him." Put the character in the subject, and leave "some force" alone.

## What the framework does not account for

**1. Narration in borrowed voices.** Examples: "as she said of herself, intellectual"; "it wouldn't be amiss"; "the lower race"; "It seemed to him that he had been so schooled by bitter experience."
- **What it does to a reader:** the narrator speaks in the words of the wife, of Gurov and of the town. The irony lives in the gap between the speaker and the judge. The reader hears two people in one line and sides with neither.
- **What the skill has:** its mindset dial assumes a single speaker. It has no measure of how close the narration sits to a character.
- **Verdict: inside the model, under where meaning goes.** A borrowed phrase is a manifold with exactly two readings, the character's and the narrator's. A section on "whose words these are" would fit. That section would also explain item 3.

**2. Renaming as approach.** "a new person" becomes "a fair-haired young lady," then "the lady with the dog."
- **What it does to a reader:** each name records how much is known, moving from rumour to sight to epithet. The renaming is the ledger moving.
- **What the skill has:** a flat rule, "one thing, one name," which would close this.
- **Verdict: inside the model.** An exception is needed: rename when the reader's knowledge of the thing changes, and treat each new name as a payment.

**3. A colon reveal that pays small on purpose.** "a new person had appeared on the sea-front: a lady with a little dog."
- **What it does to a reader:** the reveal is phrased as gossip and delivers an ordinary woman. The deflation characterises the town and sets the scale of the story.
- **What the skill has:** it lists the colon reveal as unearned weight, full stop.
- **Verdict: inside the model** once item 1 exists. Unearned weight spoken in a character's or a crowd's voice reads as that voice's failing, not the writer's.

**4. "And then" in iterative summary.** "And afterwards he met her in the public gardens and in the square several times a day."
- **What it does to a reader:** sameness is the content: "always the same béret … always the same white dog." The "and" accumulates, and that repetition is what produces the town's nickname.
- **What the skill has:** Parker and Stone's rule, which is about beats. Summary of repetition is not a beat, and the skill has no clause for it.
- **Verdict: inside the model, under tempo.** In summary, "and" is honest when the repetition is itself the point.

**5. Rhythm that carries a character's state.** "…and knew what to say to them and how to behave; and he was at ease with them even when he was silent."
- **What it does to a reader:** the sentence spills on "and … and" with the same ease it describes. The reader feels his ease, not their own emotion.
- **What the skill has:** rhythm carries the reader's feeling and the voice follows the writer's state. It has no slot for a third party's state.
- **Verdict: inside the model** with one added line: rhythm can enact the state of the person being described.

**6. A reading that depends on the reader's world.** "used phonetic spelling."
- **What it does to a reader:** someone who knows what that habit signalled about a person hears pretension. Someone who doesn't hears only an oddity, and the line's second sense never opens.
- **What the skill has:** nothing in the ledger or the glance can see whether the reader still holds the key. The rare-word rule doesn't fit, because the words are common and the context is what is missing.
- **Verdict: outside the model.** This is a fact about the reader's knowledge, not about the page or the placement of meaning.

**7. Proper nouns that pay nothing.** "Sitting in Verney's pavilion."
- **What it does to a reader:** the name raises no promise, yet it makes the place real and buys the trust that lets the later irony land.
- **What the skill has:** no account of detail that is not a sign.
- **Verdict: partly inside, under what the reader is owed.** The skill's "concede before you are forced" treats trust as a currency, so a section on specificity as credibility could be added.
