# Analysis

## 1. The purpose, from the evidence

**Genre: a scene, opening a story.** The evidence is quoted dialogue, past tense ("said his lady to him one day"), named characters and a place. The first two paragraphs are in a different register, a narrator's general claim before any scene begins. So the piece opens like an essay and turns into a scene at ¶3.

- **Dial:** the table gives a scene 4 to 5. As written it runs 3 to 4. The second sense of ¶1 is paid one paragraph later (dial 3). Its full completion is held to the last line (dial 4).
- **Rules that turn with the genre:** the reader chose to stay, so withholding is allowed, and the piece does withhold (the man's name, his state). Fragments and quick exchanges are earned by the scene. A claim in line one is a post's move; here it is the first raise of a scene, and it is not meant straight.
- **Form:** the dialogue follows the musical sentence closely. It states (¶3), restates from a step closer (¶5, ¶7), floods (¶11), fragments and accelerates (¶12 to ¶16), then cadences (¶17).

**Mindset of the draft: calm, amused, detached. How far gone: 2.**
- The narration uses long balanced sentences and a hedge ("some one or other").
- The narrator's one comment is cut to four words ("This was invitation enough.").
- Nothing is mocked outright. The amusement lives in word choice ("rightful property") and in what is left to the reader.

No target mindset was given, so this is the help mode. Three states the piece could present instead, each rewriting ¶1:

- **Amused, further gone (4):** "Every family for miles knows what a rich single man wants; he will be the last to be told."
- **Scornful (3):** "A rich man takes a house, and the neighbours have him married before his servants arrive."
- **Anxious, the families' own state (3):** "A single man with a fortune has taken the house, and there are daughters in every family, and only one of him."

## 2. The glance

The script is not available in this session, so these counts were done by hand at roughly 313 words in 17 paragraphs (16 without the illustration).

- **First paragraph:** 23 words, one sentence, about 7% of the piece. The eye takes it whole. The opening's only debt is to ¶2, and ¶2 pays it.
- **Spread:** from 1 word ("Bingley.") to 78 words (¶11).
- **Longest block:** ¶11, a single 78-word sentence. It is the only wall, placed mid-page, where the reader is already engaged.
- **The second paragraph:** 48 words in one sentence, about four to five lines on a phone. That is the skill's "next wall" after a one-line opening. It is borderline at the glance and paid on the read (see ¶2 below).
- **One-line paragraphs:** 13 of 17. By the skill's rule ("stops working at three on a page") this hums. On the page it reads as dialogue turn-taking, not emphasis. See the final section.
- **Repeated openings:** ¶3 and ¶17 both open "My dear Mr. Bennet." That is the bracket of the scene, not a tic. ¶4 and ¶6 both open "Mr. Bennet," and the repetition is his silence restated.
- **Formatting:** no lists, bold, or subheads. The illustration block is the only foreign shape.

**What a reader sees before reading:** a short claim, a longer one, then a fast column of talk with one long speech in the middle. It looks easy to enter and quick to move through.

## 6. The topic position

- **¶2 is the one paragraph a reader may read twice.** The known-new chain holds: it opens on "such a man," which ¶1 just paid. The stumble has two other causes:
  - "However" briefly parses as the connective ("However, …").
  - The subject, "this truth," arrives at word 20.
  - The paragraph still ends on its most changed words, "their daughters."
  - **Fix:** none worth the cost. The parse resolves in two words, and the periodic sentence is what delivers "property."
- **¶11 holds its chain** despite its length: Mrs. Long (¶5), then Netherfield (¶3), then the young man, Monday, possession, and the servants.
- **¶3 opens on new matter.** That is allowed at the frame edge, where the essay register becomes the scene.

## 7. Accidental readings

- **Pointer across the caption.** "This was invitation enough." Now "This" can point at the illustration rather than at ¶8. Fix: move the illustration.
- **A thing renamed.** "said his lady to him" (¶3), then "his wife" (¶7, ¶17). Fix: "his wife" both times. She is never named in the piece, only placed by marriage. That fits the subject, so keep the relational name but make it one name.
- **The connective that isn't.** "However little known…" (¶2) briefly reads as the connective. Leave it (see §6).
- **A chosen manifold, not an accident.** "in want of a wife" carries both lacks and desires, and the piece uses both. Keep it.
- **A chosen manifold, not an accident.** "the surrounding families": surrounding as nearby, and as encircling. The second reading helps "rightful property." Keep it.
- **Period words.** "let," "chaise and four," "Michaelmas" are common in the story's world and rare to a present-day reader. No fix inside the piece (see the final section).
- **Unattributed lines.** ¶12, ¶14 and ¶16 have no speaker tag. With two speakers alternating, they stay clear. No fix.

## 8. Unearned weight

- **The importance claim, earned.** "It is a truth universally acknowledged…" On pattern this is the importance claim and the fake-profound line, placed first. On the ledger it is earned: ¶2 reverses it within one sentence, and ¶17 completes it. Fix: none. It passes the ledger, not the pattern.
- **The adverb that props.** "cried his wife, impatiently." The question already shows the impatience, and the stress position is spent on the qualifier. Fix: cut "impatiently."
- **The caption.** "He came down to see the place." It promises a scene image and pays a line the reader has not reached. Fix: move it after ¶11, or after ¶17.
- **Checked and not found:** no recap, colon reveal, denied reading, three by reflex, dash or swear. ¶11's run of "that" clauses has as many items as Mrs. Long's report, so it is not a three by reflex.

## 9. The three changes that would pay the most

1. **Move the illustration** (["He came down to see the place"]). It pays ¶11's line early and cuts "This" off from its referent. Place it after ¶11 or at the end.
2. **Cut "impatiently"** ("cried his wife, impatiently."). The line already shows it. End ¶7 on the question.
3. **One name for her** ("said his lady to him"). Use "his wife," as in ¶7 and ¶17.

The piece already pays almost everything it raises. These three are small, and only the first is more than polish.

## What the framework does not account for

- **Irony: the narrator disowns its own sentence.** "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife."
  - **Effect on the reader:** they catch, by the end of ¶2 at the latest, that the narrator does not believe it. "Universally" overstates, and "rightful property" shows whose want it really is. The pleasure is being let in on the joke.
  - **What the skill sees:** an importance claim, and nothing to say about stance. The mindset dial measures the writer's state, not the writer's agreement with their claim.
  - **Verdict: inside the model** (where meaning goes) if a section is added. Irony is a raise whose plain reading the voice disowns. It is earned only when a reversal pays it within reach, and the cue is register or hyperbole.
- **Character voice versus narrator voice.** Two lines show it:
  - ¶11 ends "…by the end of next week." The skill says never end on setting. Here the character spends her stress on servants and Michaelmas and leaves out the name and marital state. That omission is the raise ¶12 and ¶14 pay, and it is the joke.
  - ¶16, "How so? how can it affect them?", is a question the asker and the reader both know the answer to. The skill would call it rhetorical and weak. In a character's mouth it is the character.
  - **What the skill misses:** its rules for stress and questions assume the writer is speaking. It has no way to say a character misplaces weight on purpose and the writer's stress falls on the next line.
  - **Verdict: inside the model** (the ledger handles it once the omission is marked as the raise). A section on voices other than the writer's would need adding.
- **Who gets quotation marks.** "Mr. Bennet replied that he had not." / "Mr. Bennet made no answer."
  - **Effect on the reader:** his voice is reported while hers is quoted. The reader hears his reticence as a silence in the page itself, so when he does speak (¶8), the dry line lands harder.
  - **What the skill sees:** it has tempo (scene versus summary) but no idea of direct versus reported speech as a choice about whose voice is heard.
  - **Verdict: inside the model** (where meaning goes). It is a cousin of the withheld word, applied to a whole voice.
- **Comedy as a feeling.** "You want to tell me, and I have no objection to hearing it." / "This was invitation enough."
  - **Effect on the reader:** amusement is this piece's main feeling. It comes from deadpan, from the beat before the dry line, and from understatement after a flood.
  - **What the skill misses:** its rhythm list covers urgency, fear, panic, ecstasy, grief, love, ease and awe. Nothing covers comic timing.
  - **Verdict: inside the model** (rhythm is emotion; the land punch is close). An entry for it would need adding.
- **Dialogue paragraphing.** 13 of 17 paragraphs are one line.
  - **What the skill sees:** its rule that one-line paragraphs stop working at three would mark this page as humming.
  - **What the reader sees:** new speaker, new paragraph, and reads the column as turn-taking, not emphasis.
  - **Verdict: inside the model** (what the page looks like). It needs an exemption: a convention the reader already knows carries no emphasis.
- **Period distance.** "a chaise and four," "before Michaelmas."
  - **Effect on the reader:** the skill's "common word for the common thing" assumes writer and reader share a vocabulary. These words were common when written and make a present-day reader stop on the word.
  - **What the skill misses:** the model can say the reader is owed the common word, but it has no time axis to say which reader. The writer cannot fix this.
  - **Verdict: partly outside the model.** It belongs to whoever presents the text (a gloss or a note).
- **Apparatus from another hand.** "[Copyright 1894 by George Allen.]"
  - **What the skill sees:** the model explains the effect (a spoiler, a broken referent, the only "and then" seam).
  - **What the skill misses:** every fix it gives assumes the writer controls the page. Captions and editors' matter come from someone else, and the skill has no category for text the writer did not write.
  - **Verdict: outside the model** as written.
