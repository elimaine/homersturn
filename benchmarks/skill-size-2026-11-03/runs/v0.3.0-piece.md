
Winter took the low country first, and by the turn of the year the granary under the chapel held nothing but its own echo. The mice counted what was left, first in grains, then in days, and at last in the names of those who would not see the thaw. There were not many of any of the three.

The grain had gone up the hill, to the house on the hill, to Lord Soft. "Count it twice," he told his collectors, settling deeper into himself. "A kingdom is only the sum of what a wise lord keeps." He was disgustingly soft to look at. Dust-grey, plump as a folded cushion, a creature you wanted to touch until you saw the small neat scales on his grain-ledger and understood that the softness was the point: nothing sharp had ever once had to touch him. When a collector came back light, Lord Soft did not shout. He struck the name from the ledger, and the family it belonged to, and ate.

Wick found the sword where the chapel floor had rotted through. "Well," she said to it, the way you greet a stranger who has sat down at your table, "you're too big." It was a sword to her the way a river is a road to a beetle — a shard of old steel, a needle longer than she was and lighter than she had feared. She did not know how a sword was used; no one she had ever known had owned one. She did not feel chosen. She felt the cold of it come up through her paws, and that was enough to be going on with.

No one sent her. That is the part they leave out when they tell it now, and the part I keep in. No bell rang. No elder laid a paw on her head and called her the one. She went up the hill because the hill was where the grain was, and because someone had to be the mouse who would not sit and count the days down to the last of them.

Lord Soft laughed when he saw the size of her. He was still laughing when she crossed his floor. A soft thing never learns what a small thing can do with its whole weight set behind a single point, and he learned it late, and he learned it badly. The sword was far too big for her. She used all of it.

The grain came back down the hill in the arms of mice who, that same morning, had believed there was nothing to be done. That is the whole of it. Not that a sword lay waiting under the floor — there are swords under every floor, splinters and needles and shards of forgotten steel, and most of them rot there in the dark. A kingdom is only ever the sum of what one small thing is willing to carry up a hill.
