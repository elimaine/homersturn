
The needle lay in the trampled wheat where a soldier had dropped it, and it was exactly the right size for a small thing to die holding. Pip picked it up anyway, and the weight of it put him on his knees. He got up.

Vane had come in the spring, soft as a rumor, with fur you wanted to touch and a voice that never rose. By autumn he held every grain of the winter seed behind one oak door. He did not eat it; a chinchilla his size could not have eaten a tenth of it. He counted it. Each evening he walked the dark aisles of the granary and read aloud the names of the families who would go hungry that year — not as a threat, and not in anger. For the pleasure of it, the way a kinder lord reads poems before sleep. The mothers outside the wall learned to put their children to bed before he reached the letter of their name.

Pip was not brave, and he would have told you so himself. He was the size of a man's thumb. The sword was the size of Pip. The walk from his hole in the south field to the granary door was the longest thing he knew, longer than winter, longer than hunger, and he had measured both.

He went at dusk. He dragged the needle through the stubble, stopping when the owls called, moving when they stopped. His arms shook. Twice he nearly turned back. He did not turn back.

Vane was reading the H names when the needle came out of the dark. It went through the soft paw and into the oak and held, and the lord who never raised his voice raised it now, pinned to the door, screaming against the long list of everyone he had starved.

Pip did not make a speech; his arms were still shaking. He climbed the lock and turned the key. All night the mice of the lower fields came and carried the winter seed out in their arms, past the soft thing nailed to the door, and not one of them stopped to read its name. They had the names by heart. They would feed their children from that granary in the morning and never tell them whose needle it was.

The needle stayed in the oak, too big ever to be a mouse's sword again, and nobody moved it. Pip walked home the long way, through the south field in the first grey light, and for the first time he could remember, the longest thing he knew felt small.
